## Complication

The EU is rewriting its merger rules during 2026, changing how competitor deals get reviewed. The Commission [published draft Merger Guidelines](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_26_918/IP_26_918_EN.pdf) on 30 April 2026. The final text is unconfirmed. The draft gives more weight to scale and efficiencies, but offers no guarantee. Tighter FDI screening compounds the shift. The [revised regulation applies from 17 January 2028](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/competition-blog/the-new-foreign-investment-screening-regulation-from-trilogues-to-the-official-journal/), so national regimes will differ for most of the window. Rates add pressure. The ECB held at 2.50% on 29 October 2026, and [markets priced a 67% chance of a December hike](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/29/european-central-bank-interest-rates.html). Overlap with any target is still unknown. An in-depth review would add up to [90 working days](https://competition-policy.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2021-02/merger_control_procedures_en.pdf). Below EU thresholds, national authorities such as the CMA can still call in deals. Strategic buyers also [paid more than financial investors](https://proventis.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/2026-02-02_MA-Facts_Logistics_H2-final.pdf) in H2 2025. A competitor deal now means committing capital before the review standard settles. This note resolves that tension.

## Answer

The board should approve an 8–10 week diagnostic and a European partnership with a call option now, deferring any control acquisition.

The partnership tests the European revenue thesis with limited capital and no control premium. The diagnostic settles segment, overlap, FSR, FDI and call-in exposure first. This avoids integration costs that [average 120 percent of run-rate synergies](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organizational-performance/our-insights/eight-basic-beliefs-about-capturing-value-in-a-merger) and an in-depth merger review of [90 working days](https://competition-policy.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2021-02/merger_control_procedures_en.pdf).

Move to control only with a committed multi-deal programme and a dedicated M&A capability.

Price competition and rising rates will erode control-deal value over the next 12–24 months. In H2 2025 logistics M&A, [strategic buyers offered a higher valuation than financial investors.](https://proventis.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/2026-02-02_MA-Facts_Logistics_H2-final.pdf) Benchmarks are thin. Debt costs add pressure: [money markets priced in a 67% chance of a December hike](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/29/european-central-bank-interest-rates.html).

A partnership with a call option should test the European thesis before any control bid. It limits capital at risk, avoids a control premium and stays reversible. Joint ventures carry risk. It adds that infrequent, large deals tend to hurt value creation. A one-off acquisition falls outside that evidence. The board should approve control deals only with a funded multi-deal programme and a dedicated M&A team.

## Adversarial Critique

### CRIT-001 — hidden-assumption (critical)

**Steelmanned position:** The So What section tells the board to approve OPT-A now. But the brief never establishes why the company wants Europe or what an acquisition is meant to achieve. Scoping lists 'that Europe is a strategic priority' and the strategic objective as unvalidated assumptions. The requester-provided section says segment, footprint, objective, budget, leverage tolerance and hurdle rate are all missing. The Answer admits value creation 'stays unquantified'. Without a defined objective you cannot tell whether a bolt-on beats a partnership or organic build, because each option solves a different problem: market access, capability, scale or defence. Approving a specific transaction structure before the objective is set is the classic strategy-follows-deal failure. A hostile reviewer would say the only defensible recommendation today is to approve a time-boxed diagnostic, not OPT-A.

**Implication if true:** The recommendation should change from 'approve OPT-A' to 'approve an 8-week diagnostic and option-selection gate'. OPT-A, OPT-C and OPT-D should stay as equal candidates until the strategic objective and financial constraints are supplied.

**Suggested revision:** Rewrite So What and Answer so that the board approves the diagnostic (objective, segment, overlap, FSR/FDI audit, leverage envelope) as the decision. Choose between OPT-A, OPT-C and OPT-D only once the gate completes.

### CRIT-002 — weak-source (material)

**Steelmanned position:** OPT-A's main evidence is McKinsey's programmatic M&A research, but the brief misapplies it. McKinsey defines programmatic M&A as more than two small or mid-sized deals a year, sustained over years. The 30% figure is a classification boundary, not a causal size cap. One deal by a company with no proven M&A capability and no committed pipeline is what McKinsey calls 'selective' M&A, a category it reports underperforms. The research also says the approach only works if M&A is treated as an enduring capability. So the evidence supports OPT-A only if the board commits to a multi-year, multi-deal programme with a dedicated M&A function. The brief does not ask for that commitment.

**Implication if true:** Taken alone, one gated bolt-on gets no support from the programmatic evidence and may fall into the underperforming 'selective' category. OPT-A is only well supported if paired with a funded multi-deal programme and an in-house M&A capability.

**Suggested revision:** Either add a board commitment to a multi-year programme (deal cadence, pipeline, dedicated M&A team) as a fifth gate, or drop the programmatic citation as evidence for a single deal and stop presenting the 30% figure as a value-protecting cap.

**Counter-evidence:** [Repeat performance: The continuing case for programmatic M&A](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/repeat-performance-the-continuing-case-for-programmatic-m-and-a)

### CRIT-003 — overconfidence (material)

**Steelmanned position:** The brief says a mid-sized target means 'fewer national filings' and that regulators 'will probably clear' the deal. It rates RISK-001 residual risk as low. The logic may run the other way. A deal below EU Merger Regulation thresholds loses the EU one-stop shop and becomes subject to several national regimes in parallel. A growing number of Member States can also call in deals that fall below their notification thresholds. The 'more than 90% clear in Phase I' figure covers all EU cases, most of them non-horizontal or simplified, so it is not a base rate for a horizontal competitor deal whose overlap is unknown. The low residual rating rests on a mitigation that simply avoids high-overlap targets, which is circular.

**Implication if true:** OPT-A's regulatory advantage over OPT-B is smaller than claimed. A mid-sized deal could face a fragmented, multi-clock national process with call-in risk, and RISK-001 residual risk should be medium, not low.

**Suggested revision:** Change OPT-A's regulatory tradeoff to flag multi-jurisdiction and call-in exposure for below-EUMR deals, add a call-in screen to the country-by-country filing map, and raise RISK-001 residual risk to medium until the overlap analysis is done.

**Counter-evidence:** [Catch me if you can (Part II) - below-threshold merger enforcement in the EU continues to evolve - WorthWeil Antitrust](https://antitrust.weil.com/catch-me-if-you-can-part-ii-below-threshold-merger-enforcement-in-the-eu-continues-to-evolve/)

### CRIT-004 — internal-contradiction (material)

**Steelmanned position:** The brief asks for urgency because 'scarce targets' will go to rivals, and also tells the company to bid on cost synergies only while admitting that strategic rivals pay more. Those two positions cannot both hold. If targets are scarce and rivals price in revenue synergies, a cost-only ceiling will lose most competitive auctions, so OPT-A's expected outcome is mostly the OPT-C fallback. If targets are not scarce, the urgency case against OPT-C and OPT-D falls away. The brief's own research finding says most logistics deals were small or mid-cap, which points to plenty of mid-sized targets rather than scarcity. Either way, the speed-and-control advantage claimed for OPT-A is overstated.

**Implication if true:** In practice OPT-A may be a slow, costly route to OPT-C, with advisory and diligence costs spent on lost auctions. The 'doing nothing hands scarce targets to rivals' argument in So What then loses force.

**Suggested revision:** Add an explicit estimate of win probability under cost-only pricing and of the cost of failed processes, and remove or evidence the 'scarce targets' claim, since the cited finding notes most deals are small or mid-cap.

**Counter-evidence:** [M&A FACTS](https://proventis.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/2026-02-02_MA-Facts_Logistics_H2-final.pdf)

### CRIT-005 — dismissed-option (material)

**Steelmanned position:** OPT-C is ranked below OPT-A mainly because 40–60% of joint ventures miss their objectives, a figure taken from an untrusted-host blog. The brief applies no matching failure rate to acquisitions. The most widely cited figure puts acquisition failure at 70–90%. On a like-for-like comparison, a JV with a call option has a similar or better chance of success, much lower capital at risk, no control premium, no union trigger and high reversibility. It also tests the revenue thesis before the company pays for it. Holding OPT-C to a failure-rate standard that OPT-A is never held to is selective use of evidence.

**Implication if true:** Without the asymmetric failure-rate argument, OPT-C is at least as defensible as OPT-A on risk-adjusted grounds, especially while the strategic objective and leverage tolerance are unknown. It should be co-recommended or made the first step.

**Suggested revision:** Put comparable failure-rate evidence for acquisitions and JVs side by side in Supporting Arguments, and re-rank OPT-C on that basis instead of treating it only as a fallback.

**Counter-evidence:** [The Big Idea: The New M&A Playbook](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-big-idea-the-new-ma-playbook)

### CRIT-006 — dismissed-option (minor)

**Steelmanned position:** The brief dismisses OPT-B partly on the claim that integration costs average 120% of run-rate synergies, citing older McKinsey material. The closest sector precedent contradicts this. DSV booked about DKK 4.5bn of transaction and integration costs in 2025 against a DKK 9bn annual synergy target. It is integrating Schenker in about 20 months and reports low attrition among large customers. For a capable acquirer, a scale deal in a fragmented segment may create more value than a string of bolt-ons that each carry fixed deal costs.

**Implication if true:** OPT-B should be rated as conditionally acceptable for an acquirer with proven integration capability, not 'not-recommended'. This does not change the current recommendation given the unverified M&A capacity.

**Suggested revision:** Replace the generic 120% integration-cost figure with sector precedent such as DSV/Schenker, and make OPT-B conditional on verified integration capability rather than dismissing it outright.

**Counter-evidence:** [FULL-YEAR 2025 RESULTS DSV A/S Conference call](https://investor.dsv.com/static-files/230e1334-a09f-4ca3-8a3e-0ac0c16e57a5)

**Evidence:** A Skadden analysis says the draft changes how the Commission assesses killer acquisitions, ecosystems, dynamic competition, scale benefits and efficiencies.
