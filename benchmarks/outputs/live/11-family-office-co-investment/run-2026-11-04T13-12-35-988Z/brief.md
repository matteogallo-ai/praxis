### CRIT-002 — hidden-assumption (critical)

**Steelmanned position:** The case for OPT-A over OPT-B rests on one assumption: that a capped minority ticket gives the family leverage to win a longer timetable, licence-linked tranching, tag-along rights and a written exit analysis. That vote anchors the council toward completing the deal and hands the advisor a signal of commitment, with no extra protection to show for it.

**Implication if true:** OPT-A has no real advantage over a structured deferral, and it adds the cost of anchoring. The recommendation should then be a deferral that sets out its conditions, with decline as the default if the lead refuses.

### CRIT-004 — missing-perspective (material)

**Steelmanned position:** The brief never asks why this allocation reached the family at all. When a deal comes through an external advisor rather than an established relationship with a lead investor, the family may be seeing deals that better-placed investors passed on: a classic lemons problem. OPT-D is rejected on the grounds that the company 'has not been checked'. But for an unsolicited deal from an advisor whose pay depends on completion, the starting expectation should be below average quality. The burden of proof belongs on the deal, not on declining it.

**Implication if true:** Declining is a reasonable default for advisor-sourced co-investments, not a costly loss of option value. That weakens the dismissal of OPT-D. Note that Braun, Jenkinson and Schemmerl (2020) found no adverse selection in a larger GP-sourced sample, so the evidence is mixed.

**Suggested revision:** Add a condition requiring the advisor and lead to explain in writing why the allocation is being offered, which other investors were approached or declined, and the lead's own ticket size relative to its fund.
